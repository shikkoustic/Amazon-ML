01

What the metric actually rewards

The score is F0.5 computed per Source 1 entity, then averaged. Three consequences drive every decision below, and getting them wrong costs more than any modelling choice.

Precision counts double. For an entity with two true matches, finding both plus one wrong scores 0.714 — worse than finding one and stopping, which scores 0.833. Guessing extra matches is actively penalised.

Partial recall is cheap. Missing one of three matches still scores 0.909 for that entity. This matters enormously for how many candidates are worth generating.

Singletons are free points. 5.58% of entities have no match at all. Predicting nothing for them scores a full 1.0; a single wrong guess scores 0.0.

The decision threshold that follows from this is 0.67, not the 0.5 a classifier defaults to. That figure was derived from the metric before any model existed, and the trained model later confirmed it exactly.

Verified · 0 exceptions

Matched records always share a country

Zero cross-country matches in the whole training set. Partitioning candidate search by country is therefore free rather than a trade-off, and it handles the unseen country automatically because the rule is “same country”, not “US or India”.

Verified · 0 exceptions

Matches are strictly one-to-many

Total matched-id slots 7,638,365 equals distinct matched ids 7,638,365. No Source 2 or Source 3 record is ever claimed by two Source 1 entities. The task is an assignment problem, not independent pair classification.

The reachability ceiling was misleading, and catching that mattered

Name or address similarity can reach 99.998% of true pairs (stable across five random seeds, standard deviation 0.002%). That number assumes exhaustive comparison. Under real top-K ranking against millions of same-country records, name-only blocking collapses from a 91.5% ceiling to 64.8% realised. A true match can be perfectly visible and still sit below thousands of businesses with similar names.

Transliteration is load-bearing, not decoration

Ordering inside normalisation turned out to matter as much as the steps themselves. Stripping legal suffixes before collapsing transliteration artefacts left a Latin record without private limited while its Indic counterpart still carried praaivett limittedd — the two sides normalised asymmetrically, dragging down exactly the pairs transliteration exists to rescue. Reversing the order lifted cross-script similarity from 0.000 to 0.16–0.27.

The single largest improvement

Indexing name and address as one combined string. Separate passes cannot express that a record agreeing on both fields beats one agreeing on either alone, which is precisely what top-K ranking was discarding. The combined field alone reaches 93.4% recall from twenty candidates per entity — better than the name-plus-address union at the same K, with half as many candidates.

Why the candidate set is deliberately small

Because F0.5 averages per entity, pair recall is a poor guide to the score. Going from twenty candidates per entity to 523 — twenty-six times the work — buys +0.018 of ceiling. Since the matcher will fall well short of its ceiling regardless, a smaller set that allows fast iteration is worth more than the last fraction of recall.

Throughput decided the final shape

Query rate turned out to be governed by how many character n-grams each record carries, not by K or by any similarity floor — varying either left the rate unchanged, because the cost sits in the sparse dot products rather than in selecting the top K. Pruning n-grams that appear in more than 5% of records roughly doubles throughput for 0.0005 of ceiling. Pruning at 0.5% is thirty times faster but costs seven points, so the useful knee is sharp and worth finding.

At the measured 90 queries per second the full test run is about sixteen hours, past the twelve-hour session limit, so it runs as two jobs (Source 2, then Source 3) whose outputs merge.

Corrected

The first score reported was wrong, and inflated

Initial evaluation gave macro F0.5 of 0.9468. That figure was an artefact: training downsampled negatives, so each validation entity faced a fraction of its candidates and had correspondingly fewer chances to attract a false positive. That flatters precision, which is the thing F0.5 weights most heavily. Re-measured against every candidate blocking produces, the honest figure is 0.9024. The gap was 0.044 — larger than most of the improvements under consideration.

05

Ideas tested and rejected

Each of these was proposed, implemented, measured, and dropped. They are listed because the numbers that killed them are as useful as the ones that survived.

Rejected · no effect

Exploiting the one-to-many constraint

Since no record may be claimed by two entities, conflicting predictions are provably wrong without consulting the model — free precision, in theory. In practice, at any useful threshold the model produces zero conflicts (0 of 12,679 predicted pairs at threshold 0.90). The constraint is real and completely non-binding.

Rejected · underperforms

Rank-aware thresholds

The break-even confidence for adding another match genuinely does rise with how many are already predicted — from 0.44 for the first of five to an 0.80 asymptote. Implemented, it scored 0.9038 against 0.9024 for a flat cut. A gain of 0.0014 is not worth the added complexity.

Rejected · costs recall

Short-circuit routing on exact matches

Skipping fuzzy search once an exact match is found assumes one match per entity. The data says otherwise: entities average 3.46 matches and reach 11. An entity with an exact Source 2 hit and a badly mangled Source 3 hit would silently lose the second.

Rejected · backwards

Intersecting two retrieval methods

Keeping only pairs found by both sparse and dense retrieval discards exactly the candidates that one method found and the other missed, which is the entire reason for running two. Blocking takes the union; agreement between methods belongs downstream as a feature.

Rejected · wrong structure

Union-Find for transitive candidates

The underlying idea is sound — reaching a Source 3 record through a Source 2 bridge. Union-Find computes unbounded transitive closure and cannot be stopped after one hop, so a single bad link merges two unrelated clusters. Under a precision-weighted metric that is the worst available failure mode. A bounded one-hop expansion gets the same benefit with a hard cap.

Diagnosed, then fixed

The model's scores were not probabilities

Training ran at 16.8% positives against a true rate of 2.6%, leaving the model badly overconfident: a score of 0.6 corresponded to a 16% chance of being correct. This is why the theory-derived thresholds failed — they assume the score means P(correct). A prior-shift correction restores calibration (p∈[0.7,0.9) now maps to an actual rate of 0.818) and puts the optimal flat threshold at 0.67, matching the value derived from the metric beforehand.

06

France, which cannot be measured

France is 15% of the test set and appears nowhere in training, so its recall is inferred rather than measured. Three pieces of indirect evidence, stated as such:

  • The configuration choice transfers. Configurations rank almost identically by US and by India recall — 86.8% pairwise ordering agreement, identical top four. The choice is not country-specific.
  • The hard case is India, not France. The feared failure mode, an unreadable writing system, is an India problem that transliteration solves. France is Latin-script, and the US column is healthier than the India column on every name signal.
  • Candidate counts look normal. On real test data France receives 127.3 candidates per entity against 124.7 for India and 129.0 for the US, with no empty normalised keys. Nothing is failing silently.

Country is used only as a partition key and is never one-hot encoded, so a new label needs no code change.

Where the remaining 0.085 is

Current honest score 0.904 against a blocking ceiling of 0.989. The decision rule is not the constraint — three separate rule changes moved it by under 0.002 combined. The gap is the model's ability to separate. In priority order:

Next

Relative features

Every feature so far judges a pair in isolation. A similarity of 0.7 means very different things depending on whether the entity's best candidate scores 0.72 or 0.98. Rank within entity, gap to the top candidate, and ratio to the entity's mean encode the competition between candidates directly, which is what a per-entity metric rewards.

A dedicated singleton decision

At the operating threshold, 85% of singletons are correctly left empty. Each of the remaining 15% costs a full 1.0 on that entity. “Does this entity match anything at all” is a different question from “which records does it match”, and deserves its own treatment.
